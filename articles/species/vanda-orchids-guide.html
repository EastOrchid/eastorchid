Vanda Orchids: Tropical Beauty with Airborne Roots

Published: July 28, 2026 | Category: Orchid Species

Vanda orchids are among the most spectacular of all orchids. With their large, vibrant flowers in shades of blue, purple, pink, and orange — and their dramatic, airy root systems that seem to defy gravity — Vanda represents the exotic, tropical beauty that many people imagine when they think of orchids.

This guide introduces Vanda orchids, their unique characteristics, their place in Southeast Asian culture, and how to grow these magnificent plants. It is the final article in our World Orchids series, completing the transition from Chinese orchid wisdom to global orchid diversity.

The Beauty of Aerial Roots

The most distinctive feature of Vanda orchids is their root system. In nature, Vanda grows attached to trees in tropical forests, with its thick, silvery roots hanging freely in the air. These roots are covered in velamen, the same spongy tissue found on other epiphytic orchids, but in Vanda it is particularly well-developed.

The aerial roots of Vanda serve two essential functions. First, they absorb moisture and nutrients directly from the air and rain — the plant does not rely on soil or potting medium for its nutrition. Second, they anchor the plant to its host tree, allowing it to grow in the bright, airy conditions of the forest canopy.

For home growers, these aerial roots are both a spectacle and a clue to the plant's needs. Healthy Vanda roots are silvery-white when dry and turn green when wet. Brown or shriveled roots indicate insufficient humidity or poor airflow. The visible nature of the roots makes Vanda one of the most instructive orchids to grow — you can literally see whether your plant is happy.

Vanda Flowers and Varieties

Vanda produces some of the most beautiful flowers in the orchid world. The blooms are large, flat, and typically round, with a distinctive overlapping petal arrangement. Colors range from the famous blue of Vanda coerulea to rich purples, pinks, oranges, yellows, and white. Many hybrids produce flowers that are striped or checkered with contrasting colors.

A mature Vanda in full bloom is an unforgettable sight. The flower spikes can carry 6–12 individual blooms, each 8–12 centimeters across, and the flowers last 4–6 weeks. In ideal conditions, a healthy Vanda can bloom 2–3 times per year.

Vanda in Southeast Asian Culture

In Southeast Asia, Vanda orchids are deeply woven into the cultural fabric. In Thailand, Vanda is one of the most important commercial orchid genera, with millions of plants grown for the cut flower and export market. Thai Vanda hybrids are known worldwide for their vibrant colors and robust growth.

In the Philippines, several Vanda species are native and have been integrated into traditional garden culture. In Singapore, the hybrid Vanda 'Miss Joaquim' — a natural hybrid between Vanda hookeriana and Vanda teres — was chosen as the national flower in 1981, a testament to the orchid's cultural significance in the region.

Growing Vanda at Home

Vanda orchids are among the more challenging orchids for home growers, requiring specific conditions that can be difficult to replicate indoors. However, for those who can provide the right environment, they are deeply rewarding.

Light — Vanda needs very bright light — more than any other orchid in this series. A south-facing window with some direct sun is ideal. In insufficient light, Vanda will grow leaves but refuse to bloom.

Water and Humidity — In nature, Vanda roots are watered daily by rain and high humidity. In the home, mist the roots daily in warm weather, and water thoroughly 2–3 times per week. Humidity should be 60–80%. A humidity tray or room humidifier is often necessary.

Airflow — Good air circulation is essential. Vanda roots need to dry between waterings, and stagnant air can lead to rot. A ceiling fan or nearby window helps.

Temperature — Warm to hot. Vanda grows best at 22–32°C (72–90°F). It does not tolerate cold and should be protected from temperatures below 15°C (60°F).

Potting — Many Vanda growers do not use pots at all. Plants are often grown in slatted baskets or mounted on plaques, with their roots exposed. If using a pot, choose one with excellent drainage and use a coarse bark or charcoal mix.
