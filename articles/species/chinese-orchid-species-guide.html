Chinese Orchid Species: A Guide to Oriental Orchids

Published: July 28, 2026 | Category: Orchid Species

Chinese orchids — often called "oriental orchids" or "Chinese cymbidiums" — are among the most treasured plants in Eastern horticulture. Unlike the large, showy tropical orchids commonly found in Western flower shops, Chinese orchids are admired for their subtle beauty, delicate fragrance, and profound cultural significance. For over two thousand years, they have been cultivated, collected, and celebrated by gardeners, scholars, and artists throughout China and beyond.

In this guide, we introduce the five most important species of traditional Chinese orchids. Whether you are a seasoned orchid grower or a newcomer to the world of orchids, understanding these species is the first step into the rich tradition of Chinese orchid appreciation.

What Are Chinese Orchids?

The term "Chinese orchid" (国兰, guó lán, literally "national orchid") refers primarily to several species of the genus Cymbidium that are native to China and East Asia. These orchids are characterized by their relatively small flowers, graceful foliage, and most importantly, their distinctive fragrance — a subtle, complex scent that Chinese culture has celebrated for millennia.

Unlike the epiphytic tropical orchids that grow on trees, most Chinese cymbidiums are terrestrial orchids that grow in soil. They are remarkably hardy plants, adapted to temperate climates with distinct seasons. This hardiness, combined with their elegant appearance, made them ideal subjects for garden cultivation in traditional Chinese homes.

The classification of Chinese orchids is based not on botany alone but on a rich tradition of aesthetic appreciation. Over centuries, Chinese orchid enthusiasts developed sophisticated criteria for judging orchids based on flower form, color, leaf shape, and most importantly, fragrance. This tradition of orchid appreciation (赏兰, shǎng lán) is as much an art as it is a science.

Chunlan (春兰) — The Spring Orchid

Scientific name: Cymbidium goeringii

Chunlan, or Spring Orchid, is perhaps the most beloved of all Chinese orchids. As its name suggests, it blooms in early spring, often emerging from the cold earth while winter is still lingering. This timing — the orchid's willingness to bloom at the very threshold of spring — has made it a powerful symbol of hope and renewal in Chinese culture.

Chunlan flowers are typically pale green to yellowish-green, with a characteristic lip marked by deep purple spots. Each flower stem usually bears a single bloom, though occasionally two. The flowers are modest in size — rarely more than five centimeters across — but their fragrance is extraordinary. Chunlan's scent has been described as a combination of sweet honey, fresh grass, and distant flowers, all blended into a perfume that is both delicate and unforgettable.

In traditional Chinese orchid appreciation, Chunlan is considered the benchmark against which all other orchids are measured. Its leaves are narrow and arching, about 20–40 centimeters long, forming an elegant clump that is attractive even when the plant is not in bloom.

Chunlan is native to a wide area of China, from the eastern provinces of Jiangsu and Zhejiang to the mountainous regions of Yunnan and Sichuan. It grows naturally in forest understories, on shaded hillsides, and along stream banks — environments that provide the cool, well-drained conditions it prefers.

Huilan (蕙兰) — The Chinese Cymbidium

Scientific name: Cymbidium faberi

Huilan, also known as Chinese Cymbidium, is a close relative of Chunlan but with several distinct characteristics. The most noticeable difference is size: Huilan is a larger plant in almost every dimension, with taller leaves and longer flower spikes that can carry multiple blooms.

Huilan typically blooms in mid-spring, shortly after Chunlan has finished. Its flowers are similar in color to Chunlan — pale green to yellow-green — but they are carried on taller stems that rise proudly above the foliage. A mature Huilan plant can produce a flower spike of 40–60 centimeters in height, bearing 5–12 individual flowers.

The fragrance of Huilan is different from Chunlan — stronger and more penetrating, but still unmistakably the "deep fragrance" (幽香, yōu xiāng) that Chinese orchid lovers treasure. Huilan has been cultivated in China for centuries and was a particular favorite of the literati during the Ming and Qing dynasties.

Jianlan (建兰) — The Summer Orchid

Scientific name: Cymbidium ensifolium

Jianlan, or Summer Orchid, gets its name from its blooming season — it flowers during the summer months, providing color and fragrance when most other orchids are resting. This extended blooming period makes it one of the most rewarding Chinese orchids for home growers.

Jianlan is native to southern China, including Fujian Province (from which its name derives), Guangdong, and Taiwan. It prefers warmer conditions than Chunlan or Huilan and is more tolerant of heat and humidity. The flowers are similar in form to other Chinese cymbidiums but often have a more pronounced yellow or greenish coloration, and the lip markings tend to be more vivid.

One of Jianlan's most appealing qualities is its long blooming season. A well-grown plant can produce flowers from early summer well into autumn, with individual blooms lasting several weeks. Its leaves are also distinctive — broader and more upright than Chunlan, with a dark green color that remains attractive throughout the year.

Hanlan (寒兰) — The Cold Orchid

Scientific name: Cymbidium kanran

Hanlan, or Cold Orchid, is the last of the main Chinese cymbidium species to bloom, flowering in late autumn and early winter. Its name — "cold orchid" — reflects its willingness to bloom in the coldest season, a quality that has made it a symbol of perseverance and quiet strength.

Hanlan's flowers are distinguished by their narrow petals and sepals, which give them a more star-like appearance compared to the rounder flowers of Chunlan and Jianlan. The colors tend toward pale green, sometimes with a faint purplish tinge. The fragrance of Hanlan is subtle and ethereal — some describe it as having a slightly spicy note beneath the characteristic orchid sweetness.

Hanlan is native to the mountainous regions of southern China, Japan, and Korea. It is one of the more challenging Chinese orchids to cultivate, requiring cool winter temperatures and careful watering. For experienced growers, however, the reward of seeing Hanlan bloom in the depths of winter is well worth the effort.

Molan (墨兰) — The Ink Orchid

Scientific name: Cymbidium sinense

Molan, or Ink Orchid, is named for the deep, dark green of its leaves, which resemble the color of Chinese ink. It is one of the most popular Chinese orchids in modern cultivation, particularly in southern China and Taiwan, where its robust growth and spectacular flower spikes make it a favorite for exhibition and display.

Molan blooms in late winter to early spring, producing tall flower spikes that can reach 60–80 centimeters in height, bearing 10–20 individual flowers. The flowers are typically a rich maroon or dark purple, a dramatic contrast to the pale greens of other Chinese cymbidiums. This darker coloration, combined with the plant's imposing size, gives Molan a distinct presence.

In traditional Chinese culture, Molan was associated with scholarly refinement. Its dark, glossy leaves were said to resemble the ink stone of a calligrapher, and its dignified bearing was admired as an expression of cultivated character. Today, it remains one of the most widely grown Chinese orchids, both in China and in the international orchid community.

Chinese Orchids Around the World

In recent decades, Chinese orchids have gained a growing international following. Botanical gardens in Europe, North America, and Japan have developed significant collections of Chinese cymbidiums. International orchid exhibitions increasingly feature categories for Chinese orchids, and online communities have connected Chinese orchid enthusiasts across the globe.

For those new to Chinese orchids, the best advice is to start with one of the more forgiving species — Jianlan or Molan are excellent choices for beginners — and to learn the specific requirements of each species before expanding the collection. Conclusion

The five species described here represent the foundation of Chinese orchid culture. Each has its own character, its own season, and its own beauty. Learning to recognize and appreciate these differences is the beginning of a journey into the world of Chinese orchids — a world where horticulture, art, and philosophy merge into a single, enriching pursuit.

As you explore further, you will discover that behind each species lies not just botanical facts but centuries of cultural meaning.
